Delivery Phasing & Justification

How we sequence the ten modules into three phases — and, more importantly, why in this order. The sequencing is driven by dependency, regulatory non-negotiables, early proof of value, and deferring the expensive, slow, and speculative work until the foundation is validated.

The six principles behind the sequence

Every phasing decision below traces back to one of these. They are the "why" behind the "what."

01
Foundation has the highest dependency fan-out
The event spine and orchestration core sit under everything. Nothing can be built, demoed, or integrated until they exist — so they go first, full stop.
02
Compliance is a gate, not a feature
You cannot legally pilot a collections platform that can dial outside hours or contact a bankrupt customer. Compliance ships in Phase 1 — retrofitting it later is far costlier and riskier.
03
Prove value & credibility early
A buying committee funds what they can see working. Governed AI and live stakeholder surfaces land in Phase 1 so the platform is demonstrably real, not a promise.
04
Decouple from slow, external dependencies
Live core-banking integration is owned by vendor/IT timelines and bank change control — slow and political. We prove the contract with mocks in P1 so nothing else waits on it.
05
Defer scale until the shape is proven
Multi-region, autoscaling, and exactly-once guarantees optimize an architecture. Building them before the logic is validated means optimizing the wrong thing.
06
Automation needs a baseline of real data
Auto-A/B promotion, auto-retraining, and optimization loops are only meaningful once real production outcomes exist to learn from. They come last by necessity, not preference.

In scope (the Phase-1 must-do core)

Why these, why now
  1. The spine is the prerequisite for everything (Principle 1). Until events flow and journeys are durable, there is nothing for policy, AI, or UIs to attach to. It is the only thing that must be week 0.
  2. Compliance ships with — not after — the first action (Principle 2). The moment the platform can send anything, it must be unable to send a non-compliant thing. Building the gate alongside the first dispatch is an order of magnitude cheaper than bolting it on once actions already flow.
  3. AI decision capture must exist from the first AI action (Principle 3). You cannot backfill reasoning traces. If the structured-capture harness isn't there when an agent makes its first decision, that decision is unauditable forever. So it's foundational, not a later enhancement.
  4. The risk model is needed to demo and to seed Phase 2 (Principle 3). A working model proves the analytics story to the CRO and generates the very performance data the model-risk team will validate in Phase 2.
  5. Core integration is mocked, deliberately (Principle 4). We build and prove the adapter contract end-to-end against mocks so Phase 1 never blocks on the bank's core-vendor change calendar. The contract is real; only the source is simulated.
Exit criteria: the platform runs scripted, real-data journeys end-to-end; every action is compliance-gated and audited; AI decisions are captured and explainable; the risk model scores live; all five persona surfaces read live data. The buying committee sees the platform working on their portfolio.
PHASE 2
Production — Months 3–6
Goal: live integration, model-risk sign-off, resilience → production go-live for one product line
In scope
  • Live core-banking integration (real SoR)
  • Production channel vendors (SMS/email/dialer/IVR)
  • SR 11-7 model-risk artifacts + validation
  • Champion/challenger live in production
  • RBAC & SSO
  • OpenTelemetry, SLOs, alerting, runbooks
  • Scale & resilience hardening
  • BI / warehouse integration
  • Human-in-the-loop review queues
  • UDAAP/FCRA/state compliance expansion
Why these are Phase 2 — not earlier, not later
  1. Live core integration follows a proven contract (Principle 4). By Phase 2 the adapter contract is validated against mocks, so the live connection is a swap of the data source — not a discovery exercise — even though it runs on the bank's slower change calendar.
  2. Model-risk validation needs Phase-1 data. SR 11-7 sign-off requires real model performance, calibration, and stability evidence. That evidence is generated by the Phase-1 model running on real data — so validation is a Phase-2 gate before the model drives production decisions.
  3. Production-grade ops is sized to real load. SLOs, autoscaling, and DR are calibrated against observed Phase-1 throughput and failure modes — defining them earlier would be guesswork (Principle 5).
  4. Champion/challenger goes live once there's volume to split. A/B is only meaningful at production volume; Phase 1 proves the framework, Phase 2 runs it for real.
  5. Compliance breadth expands against a working gate. Reg F (the highest-risk surface) is live from Phase 1; UDAAP/FCRA/state rules extend the same proven enforcement mechanism rather than inventing a new one.
Exit criteria: the platform acts on live core data through real channels; models are MRM-approved; access is controlled; SLOs and DR runbooks are in place. Production go-live for the first product line.
PHASE 3
Scale — Months 6–12
Goal: automate, broaden across products, and reach enterprise resilience
In scope
  • Auto-promotion of winning challengers
  • Automated model retraining + drift alerting
  • Treatment / uplift optimization loop
  • Specialist agents (dispute/settlement/hardship)
  • Agent evaluation harness + model A/B
  • Omnichannel sequencing + agent desktop
  • Business-user policy authoring UI
  • Multi-product rollout
  • Master data management / entity resolution
  • Multi-region DR + autoscaling
Why these come last (by necessity, not preference)
  1. Automation requires a baseline of real outcomes (Principle 6). Auto-promotion, auto-retrain, and optimization loops learn from production results. Until Phase 2 has produced months of real outcome data, these would be automating decisions with no evidence behind them.
  2. Multi-product reuse needs one product proven first. Rolling the platform to a second and third product line is low-risk after the patterns are battle-tested on the first — and high-risk before.
  3. Enterprise resilience optimizes a known-good design (Principle 5). Multi-region DR and autoscaling are expensive to build and change. Doing them last means doing them once, against a stable architecture.
  4. Business-user authoring & specialist agents are breadth, not foundation. They multiply value once the core decisioning and governed-AI patterns are trusted in production — premature if introduced before that trust exists.
Exit criteria: strategies and models self-optimize within governance guardrails; the platform spans multiple product lines; resilience meets enterprise/regulatory standards. The platform is a self-improving, enterprise-wide capability.

Dependency graph

Modules as nodes, dependencies as arrows. An arrow A → B means B needs A — A must exist (or its data must flow) before B can work. The graph reads top-down: external inputs feed the spine, the spine enables control and intelligence, and everything surfaces through the UI while Platform Ops observes it all.

How to read it: the dark Orchestration Core is the hub — both external inputs (channels, core integration) feed it, and everything downstream depends on it. Control (strategy + compliance) sits directly on the spine; intelligence (AI, risk) and data (lakehouse) consume events and decisions; the UI reads everything; Platform Ops observes all (dashed). The deeper a node sits, the later it can start — that is the phasing, made structural.

The single most important sequencing decision: compliance and AI-decision capture are built into the foundation, not added later. Both are effectively impossible to retrofit cleanly — an un-gated action can violate regulation the moment it ships, and an AI decision made without structured capture is unauditable forever. Everything else can be layered; these two cannot.
